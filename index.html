Preface

On reflective practice

This portfolio does not set out simply to display finished artefacts. Its purpose, in keeping with the conventions of reflective practice as they are understood within communication education, is to render visible the reasoning that sat behind each piece of work: the brief as it was received, the constraints under which it was produced, the judgement calls that were made, and the learning that followed. Each entry therefore moves from description to analysis, following a structure broadly consistent with Gibbs’s (1988) reflective cycle — what happened, what it felt like to do, what can be drawn from it, and what it changes about my practice going forward — without labouring the framework so heavily that it obscures the work itself.

The six months spent with Jamaica Diaspora Magazine were conducted almost entirely online, which shaped both the character of the work I was assigned and the kind of evidence available to document it. Where a conventional internship diary might have included photographs of a newsroom or a production meeting, mine instead consists of finished digital artefacts: an article, a set of promotional thumbnails, an editorial layout produced in two iterations, and a piece of campaign design. I have treated this constraint as itself worth reflecting upon, since remote collaboration is now a routine feature of communication work rather than an exception to it.

Exhibit 01

The University of Dr Jimmy Cliff

Format: Feature article · Channel: Print and digital, Jamaica Diaspora Magazine

This feature was built around a recurring idea in Tessanne Chin’s recollection of touring with the reggae statesman Jimmy Cliff OM: that the years she spent travelling with him amounted, in her own words, to an informal education. My task was to shape the interview material she had given into a piece that would read as tribute rather than as straightforward biography, since the article was produced in the period following Cliff’s death in November 2025, at the age of eighty-one, and its tone needed to carry the weight that occasion demanded.

The principal difficulty lay in sequencing. Chin’s account moved fluidly between anecdote, technical observation of Cliff’s stagecraft, and reflection on how the experience shaped her own subsequent career, including her win on the American singing competition The Voice in December 2013. Reproducing that fluency in prose without flattening it into a list of facts required several passes at the structure before the piece held together as a single narrative arc rather than a set of loosely connected quotations.

What I take from this exercise is a firmer grasp of how music journalism functions as a form of cultural documentation. A profile of this kind is not only reporting what a source said; it is deciding, with some editorial responsibility, which parts of a life carry forward as legacy. That is a different discipline from the campaign-facing writing I had done previously, and it sharpened my sense of when a subject calls for restraint rather than embellishment.

Fig. 2 — Promotional thumbnail, video interview series.

Exhibit 02

Behind the Greatness

Format: Promotional thumbnail (video) · Channel: YouTube and social media, Jamaica Diaspora Magazine

This thumbnail supported a companion video interview to Exhibit 01, in which Chin discussed her time with Cliff on camera. Because the two pieces were released close together, the design brief was to hold a visual family resemblance to the printed feature — the same gold-on-dark palette used across the magazine’s digital output — while still functioning as a standalone piece capable of competing for attention in a crowded video feed.

The main challenge was one of hierarchy: two named individuals appear in the frame, each with a legitimate claim to prominence, and the composition had to make clear who was being interviewed and who was conducting the interview without a caption doing that work for it. I resolved this through scale and positioning rather than typography alone, giving the subject the more dynamic pose and reserving a quieter portrait treatment for the presenter.

Producing this thumbnail taught me that promotional design is a form of argument as much as a form of decoration. Every choice of crop, contrast, and type weight is implicitly making a claim about what the viewer should notice first, and that claim needs to be consistent with the editorial content it is advertising rather than more sensational than it.

Exhibit 03

Reshaping the Jamaican Economy

Format: Promotional thumbnail (video) · Channel: YouTube, Jamaica Diaspora Magazine

This piece promoted a conversation between economist Dr Kadamawe Knife and interviewer Mrs Doreen Buchanan JP, MSc, on the subject of structural change in the Jamaican economy — a considerably more technical subject than the cultural features described above, and one aimed at a diaspora audience with a professional or investment interest in Jamaica rather than a purely nostalgic one.

The design problem here was persuasive rather than purely aesthetic. An economics interview does not carry the same built-in visual drama as a music feature, so the composition needed to work harder to signal authority and relevance: a formal photograph of the speaker at a lectern, a confident colour block, and a headline treatment large enough to be legible at the small sizes at which thumbnails are typically viewed on mobile devices.

Working on this brief improved my understanding of platform-specific design constraints, particularly the practice of testing a layout at thumbnail scale rather than only at full resolution. It also reinforced that promotional design is a communication-planning exercise: colour, contrast, and framing are being used here to make a policy discussion feel accessible rather than dry.

Exhibit 04

Unshaken: How the Basi Kyokushin Hall of Fame Embodied the Spirit of the Diaspora

Format: Editorial layout, two design iterations · Channel: Jamaica Diaspora Magazine

The two layouts reproduced below are successive treatments of the same editorial, which reported on the inaugural Jamaica International Martial Arts Hall of Fame, held under the Basi Kyokushin Global Karate Organization only days before Hurricane Melissa made landfall in western Jamaica on 28 October 2025 as the most powerful storm on record to strike the island. The editorial deliberately draws a parallel between the discipline of the martial arts tradition being honoured and the resilience the country would be called upon to show in the days that followed.

Producing two iterations of the same editorial was itself instructive. The first version prioritised urgency, using a heavier sans-serif masthead and a cooler grey field appropriate to breaking coverage. On review, this register sat awkwardly against the retrospective, commemorative tone of the copy, which was reflecting on an event already past rather than reporting live. The second iteration moved to a warmer, more restrained parchment background and a serif masthead, better matched to the editorial’s function as considered commentary rather than news.

This was the most demanding brief in the portfolio, not on account of any single design decision, but because of the need to hold a long, dense body of text readable across multiple columns while giving the page enough visual authority to carry an editorial voice. It taught me to treat layout revision as an analytical exercise in matching form to register, rather than as a purely cosmetic adjustment.

Fig. 5 — Seasonal campaign visual, real estate client.

Exhibit 05

Azanell Properties International

Format: Campaign graphic · Channel: Social media, client-facing digital communication

This item represents the more explicitly commercial side of the placement. The brief was to produce a seasonal graphic for Azanell Properties International that would open the new year with goodwill messaging while still functioning as brand communication for a property development client, incorporating render imagery of the development itself beneath the festive headline.

The central tension was between warmth and credibility. A purely festive graphic risks reading as generic; a purely corporate one risks feeling cold at a moment when audiences expect a more personal tone from brands. I worked to balance the two by keeping the seasonal elements light-touch — confined to the upper register of the composition — while letting the architectural renders, which carry the aspirational quality the client wanted to project, occupy the majority of the frame.

This task sharpened my understanding of how mood, message, and market positioning interact in property marketing specifically, where the product being sold is as much a lifestyle as a physical asset. It also underlined a more general lesson about client work: a promotional graphic has to satisfy the occasion it is marking and the brand it is representing simultaneously, and neither can be allowed to overwhelm the other.

Primary source

Remembering the Legendary Jimmy Cliff

Source: Interview with Tessanne Chin, published to YouTube

This interview forms the primary research material underlying Exhibits 01 and 02 and is included here in its own right as a piece of source evidence, consistent with the requirement that transcribed or recorded material used in producing a portfolio item be identifiable and traceable to its origin.

Recorded as a tribute in the aftermath of Jimmy Cliff’s death, the conversation positions Chin as a witness to his professional discipline rather than simply as an admirer of his music. She recounts being introduced to Cliff at seventeen through the Jamaican drummer Desi Jones, a family friend, and describes the years that followed — touring internationally through the mid-2000s — as a period that instilled in her the habits of preparation, vocal preservation, and consistency that a professional touring schedule demands. She also recalls Cliff’s willingness to take public positions on political questions, including his outspoken opposition to the Iraq war and, earlier, his performances in South Africa before the end of apartheid, framing this as evidence of an artist unwilling to separate his convictions from his public role.

“I went to the University of Jimmy Cliff because my college years were spent literally on a tour bus.”

This line is the interview’s most useful moment for academic purposes, since it compresses Chin’s central argument into a single, memorable formulation: that mentorship in the creative industries is rarely formal, and is often absorbed through sustained proximity to someone practising their craft at the highest level rather than through direct instruction. That idea informed the editorial framing of Exhibit 01 directly.

Contextual notes

  • Jimmy Cliff, born James Chambers on 30 July 1944, died on 24 November 2025 at the age of eighty-one. He was invested with the Order of Merit, one of Jamaica’s highest national honours, and remains one of only two Jamaican artists inducted into the Rock and Roll Hall of Fame.
  • Tessanne Chin won the fifth season of NBC’s The Voice in the United States in December 2013, the first Jamaican performer to win a major American televised singing competition.

Synthesis

Overall reflection on the placement

Taken together, the five exhibits above span a wider range of communication practice than any one of them shows in isolation: long-form cultural writing, promotional design for video content, editorial layout revision, and commercial campaign work for an external client. What unites them is less a shared format than a shared discipline — each required me to read a brief carefully enough to identify not just what was being asked for, but what register the finished piece needed to strike, and to defend design or editorial decisions against that reading rather than against personal preference.

The remote character of the placement was, on balance, more instructive than limiting. Working without the informal cues available in a physical newsroom — overhearing a conversation, seeing a layout on someone else’s screen before it is finished — meant that written feedback, careful documentation of decisions, and a certain amount of self-directed judgement had to substitute for supervision that would otherwise have been more continuous. That is an increasingly ordinary condition of communication work, and the placement gave me direct experience of operating within it rather than simply being told that it would be necessary.

If there is a single thread that runs through the reflective commentary attached to each exhibit, it is a growing appreciation that communication practice is exercised through constraint rather than despite it: a house style, a platform’s dimensions, a subject’s cultural weight, a client’s brand guidelines. The competence I take from this placement is not simply technical fluency with the tools involved, but a more settled instinct for working productively inside those constraints — an instinct I expect to draw on directly as I move from postgraduate study into professional practice in integrated marketing communication.
